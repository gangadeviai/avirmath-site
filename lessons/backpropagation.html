What Is Backpropagation? The Chain Rule That Trains Every Neural Network

5:09 video8 sectionsFoundations · 02
In one sentence. Backpropagation is the chain rule organised so that one backward sweep gives the derivative of the error with respect to every weight at once — the gradient that gradient descent needs.
Section 1 ▶ watch 0:00

What is backpropagation?

What is backpropagation? It is the chain rule, organised so that one backward sweep gives the derivative of the error with respect to every weight in a network, all at once.

Gradient descent takes the step. Backpropagation supplies the direction. Together they are how every neural network learns.

Section 2 ▶ watch 0:18

The problem: one weight at a time

Here is the obvious way to find a slope. Nudge one weight, run the whole network again, and see how the error moved. That is one full pass, for one weight.

A thousand weights means a thousand and one passes. A billion weights means a billion and one. In nineteen seventy, Seppo Linnainmaa showed there is a better way, and in nineteen seventy-four Paul Werbos applied it to neural networks. One forward pass. One backward pass. Every derivative.

Section 3 ▶ watch 0:47

The chain rule, seen

It rests on one fact from calculus. If y depends on u, and u depends on x, then a small nudge in x is scaled once by d u by d x, and again by d y by d u. Rates multiply.

Watch the nudge travel. It enters at x, is stretched by the first slope, then by the second. The total stretch is the product. A network is just a longer chain.

Section 4 ▶ watch 1:10

The mathematics, worked by hand

Take the smallest network that has a hidden layer. Input x, weight w one, a sigmoid, weight w two, output y hat, and a squared error. Two weights. Four stages.

Forward pass, with numbers. x is 1.5, w one is 0.8, so z is 1.2. The sigmoid gives h equals 0.769. w two is -0.4, so y hat is -0.307. The target is 1. The error is 1.709.

Now backwards. The error depends on y hat through the square, so d L by d y hat is two times y hat minus y: -2.615.

y hat is w two times h, so the slope with respect to w two is that number times h: -2.01. The first weight is done.

To reach w one we must pass through h. The slope with respect to h is d L by d y hat, times w two: 1.046. Keep this number. Everything before h will reuse it.

h is the sigmoid of z, and the sigmoid's slope is sigma times one minus sigma: here 0.178. z is w one times x. Multiply the chain: 1.046, times 0.178, times 1.5. d L by d w one is 0.279.

Two derivatives, one backward sweep. Hand them to gradient descent with eta equal to 0.5: w one becomes 0.66, w two becomes 0.605, and the error falls from 1.709 to 0.312.

Section 5 ▶ watch 2:52

Why backwards

Notice what we reused. The slope at h was computed once, and every weight upstream of h multiplied by it. In a wide layer, hundreds of weights share the same number.

Call that shared quantity delta: the slope of the error with respect to a layer's inputs. Delta for one layer is the next layer's delta, pulled back through its weights, times the sigmoid slope. And the gradient of a weight matrix is simply delta times the layer's input. Three lines. Any depth.

Here is the proof you can run. Take the backward-sweep gradient, and the nudge-and-remeasure gradient, on the same surface. The arrows coincide, to six decimal places.

Section 6 ▶ watch 3:32

In industry: reading handwritten digits

In nineteen eighty-six, Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams published this in Nature, and showed that the hidden layers learn useful features on their own. Three years later, Yann LeCun's group trained a network by backpropagation to read handwritten postcodes for the United States Postal Service: 9298 digits, about one percent error.

Here is a small version. 600 noisy eight-by-eight digits, a network with one hidden layer, and nothing but the three lines you just saw. It starts at 61 percent. After 40 passes through the data it reads 91 percent correctly.

Section 7 ▶ watch 4:07

Today: autograd and the vanishing gradient

Today nobody writes these derivatives by hand. Frameworks record the forward computation as a graph and run the backward sweep automatically. The algorithm is unchanged, from two weights to hundreds of billions.

But the chain rule has a cost. Each sigmoid layer multiplies the backward signal by at most a quarter. Six layers deep, the gradient reaching the first weights is thousands of times smaller than at the output. That is the vanishing gradient, and it is why the next lessons exist: better activations, residual connections, normalisation.

Section 8 ▶ watch 4:40

Recap: what, when and how

So, what is backpropagation? The chain rule, run backwards through a network, giving every weight's derivative in one sweep.

When? Linnainmaa, nineteen seventy. Werbos, nineteen seventy-four. Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, nineteen eighty-six. How is it used in A.I.? Every training step of every network: forward pass, backward pass, gradient descent step. Next: vectors and the dot product.

Assessment

Answer each question in your own words, then reveal the model answer. Every answer comes from the lesson's verified mathematics.

What is backpropagation in simple terms?

Show answer
A way to find out, for every weight in a network, how much the error would change if that weight moved — all in one sweep from the output back to the input.

Is backpropagation the same as gradient descent?

Show answer
No. Backpropagation computes the gradient; gradient descent uses it to update the weights. One supplies the direction, the other takes the step.

Who invented backpropagation, and when?

Show answer
Reverse-mode differentiation was described by Seppo Linnainmaa in 1970. Paul Werbos applied it to neural networks in 1974, and Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams showed its power in Nature in 1986.

Why is it called back-propagation?

Show answer
Because the error signal is propagated backwards: each layer's derivative is computed from the layer after it, reusing work instead of recomputing it.

Why does backpropagation need the chain rule?

Show answer
A network is a composition of functions. The derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives of its parts, and backpropagation multiplies those parts in the cheapest order.

What is the vanishing gradient problem?

Show answer
Each sigmoid layer multiplies the backward signal by at most a quarter, so after many layers the gradient reaching the early weights is tiny. It is why ReLU, residual connections and normalisation exist.
