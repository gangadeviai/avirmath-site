What Is Gradient Descent? From Cauchy's Orbits (1847) to Training AI

5:40 video8 sectionsFoundations · 01
In one sentence. When no formula gives the answer, measure your error, find the direction in which it grows fastest, and step the opposite way; repeat. That procedure, from 1847, trains nearly every neural network today.
Section 1 ▶ watch 0:00

What is gradient descent?

What is gradient descent? It is the algorithm that lets a machine learn: measure the error, find the direction in which it falls fastest, take a step, and repeat.

It trains almost every neural network in use today, from image recognition to large language models. But it began long before computers, with a problem about the planets.

Section 2 ▶ watch 0:20

Paris, 1847: Cauchy's idea

Paris, eighteen forty-seven. Augustin-Louis Cauchy wants to compute the orbit of a body in the heavens. He has the equations. But nothing he knows will solve them exactly.

So he changes the question. Take any guess, and measure how wrong it is. One number: the error.

Picture that error for every possible guess. It forms a landscape. High where the guess is poor, low where it is good. The solution sits at the very bottom.

But Cauchy cannot see this landscape. Every single point costs him pages of arithmetic. All he can find is the slope, right where he stands.

That slope is the gradient. It points where the error grows fastest. So he steps the opposite way, and asks again.

From above, the landscape becomes a map of contour lines. Every step crosses them at right angles: the steepest way down. Step by step, the guess slides into the valley.

Cauchy wrote this up in three pages. Today it is called gradient descent.

Section 3 ▶ watch 1:23

The mathematics, step by step

Let's do it by hand. Here is a landscape we can write down: f of x, y, equals x squared plus three y squared. From above, its contours are ellipses, and the lowest point is at the origin.

Start at x equals -2.6, y equals 1.4. The error there is 12.64.

Now the slope. Differentiate with respect to x, treating y as a constant: two x. Then with respect to y: six y. Those two numbers, side by side, are the gradient.

At our point, that is -5.2, and 8.4. The error rises fastest up and to the left. So we go the other way.

How far? Multiply the gradient by a small number, eta, the learning rate, and subtract. With eta equal to 0.12, the new x is -1.98, and the new y is 0.39.

One step, and the error has fallen from 12.64 to 4.37. That is the entire algorithm. Everything else is repetition.

Section 4 ▶ watch 2:28

The learning rate: how big a step?

But that small number matters enormously. Here are four runs on the same landscape. With eta at 0.03, the steps are tiny, and it crawls.

At 0.12, it glides into the valley.

At 0.31, it overshoots the steep walls and zig-zags from side to side. And at 0.35, every step lands higher than the last. It never comes back.

The reason is exact. Along the steep direction, each step multiplies the error by one minus six eta. Keep that between minus one and one, and you converge. So eta must stay below one third: two, divided by the steepest curvature. The steepest direction sets the speed limit for everything else.

Section 5 ▶ watch 3:13

Stanford, 1960: Widrow, Hoff and stochastic gradient descent

Stanford, nineteen sixty. Bernard Widrow and his student, Ted Hoff, are building a machine that learns. They call it Adaline.

Their problem is cost. The true gradient needs the error averaged over all the data. So they make a bold simplification: estimate the gradient from a single example, take a step, and move on.

Each estimate is noisy, and the path wanders. But on average it still heads downhill, and it costs almost nothing.

They called it least mean squares. Today we call the idea stochastic gradient descent, and it is how every large model is trained.

Section 6 ▶ watch 3:50

In industry: the adaptive noise canceller

And it went straight into industry. Here is the classic case. A microphone picks up a signal we want, buried under noise that is louder than the signal itself.

A second microphone hears only the noise. An adaptive filter, with just two weights, predicts how that noise leaks into the first microphone, and subtracts it.

Its error surface is a bowl, and the bottom of the bowl is the true leak. After every single sample, the filter takes one Widrow-Hoff step.

Watch the weights walk downhill, and the signal emerge. In 4000 samples, the noise drops by 28 decibels. Nobody told the filter the answer. It found 0.8 and -0.5 on its own.

This is the mathematics inside echo cancellation on phone calls, noise-cancelling headphones, modems, and medical monitors.

Section 7 ▶ watch 4:41

Today: how gradient descent trains neural networks

Now scale it up. A neural network's error landscape is not a smooth bowl. It is rugged, and it has not two dimensions, but billions: one for every weight.

The rule does not change. Backpropagation computes the gradient for every weight at once. Small random batches of data make each step cheap, exactly as Widrow and Hoff did.

Refinements such as momentum and Adam smooth the path, and the learning rate is warmed up, then slowly decayed. But underneath, it is still Cauchy's idea.

Section 8 ▶ watch 5:13

Recap: what, when and how

So, what is gradient descent? An algorithm that reduces an error by repeatedly stepping against its gradient.

When was it invented? In eighteen forty-seven, by Cauchy. How is it used in A.I.? It adjusts the weights of a model, a little after every batch of data, until the predictions stop improving.

Next: how does a network find the gradient for a billion weights at once? That is backpropagation.

What is gradient descent in simple terms?

Show answer
A way to find the lowest point of an error landscape without seeing it: measure the slope where you stand, take a step downhill, and repeat.

Who invented gradient descent, and when?

Show answer
Augustin-Louis Cauchy described it in 1847 in a three-page note to the French Academy of Sciences, while working on equations from astronomy.

How is gradient descent used in AI and machine learning?

Show answer
A model's error is written as a function of its weights. Backpropagation computes the gradient of that error, and gradient descent nudges every weight against it after each small batch of data.

What is the learning rate?

Show answer
The number that scales each step. Too small and learning crawls; too large and the steps overshoot and diverge. For a quadratic error it must stay below 2 divided by the steepest curvature.

What is stochastic gradient descent?

Show answer
Gradient descent that estimates the gradient from one example or a small random batch instead of the whole dataset. Widrow and Hoff introduced the idea in 1960 as the LMS algorithm.

Where is gradient descent used in industry?

Show answer
In training neural networks of every kind, and, through the LMS algorithm, in echo cancellation, noise-cancelling headphones, modems and medical signal monitoring.
