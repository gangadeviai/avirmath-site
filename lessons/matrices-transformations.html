Matrices in AI: The Linear Algebra Inside Every Neural Network Layer, Attention Head and Recommender

7:07 video8 sectionsFoundations · 04
In one sentence. Every layer of a neural network is a matrix, and a matrix is a machine that moves all of space at once: its columns say where the basis arrows land, multiplying matrices is doing one move after another, the determinant is how much area the move scales, and eigenvectors are the directions it only stretches. Attention, images, web ranking, recommendation and cheap fine-tuning are all the same machine at scale.
Section 1 ▶ watch 0:00

The matrix inside every layer

Inside every neural network, between one layer and the next, sits a matrix. A vector of activations goes in, the matrix moves it, and a new vector comes out. GPT-two is a stack of hundreds of them; the model reading a C.T. scan is a stack of them too. So what does a matrix actually do? Not a table of numbers. A machine that moves all of space at once. Feed it the whole plane, and the whole plane moves, with grid lines staying straight and evenly spaced.

And the machine is easy to read. Its first column is where the arrow one, zero lands. Its second column is where zero, one lands. Know those two, and you know where everything goes.

Section 2 ▶ watch 0:40

A matrix times a vector

Take the matrix with rows two, one, and zero, one. Its columns are two, zero, and one, one. Apply it to the vector one, two. The rule says: one times the first column, plus two times the second column.

One times two, zero, is two, zero. Two times one, one, is two, two. Add them: four, two. That is where the vector lands. Watch the grid move with it: the x direction stretches to twice its length, and the y direction leans to the right.

The one property that makes all of this work is linearity. The matrix of a sum is the sum of the matrices. Scale the input, and the output scales. That is why the grid lines stay straight, and why the two columns are enough.

Section 3 ▶ watch 1:26

Multiplication is composition

Now do two moves in a row. First stretch x by two. Then rotate by ninety degrees. There is a single matrix that does both at once, and it is the product: the rotation matrix, times the stretch matrix. Read right to left, like functions.

Multiply them out. The rotation is zero, minus one, one, zero. The stretch is two, zero, zero, one. Row times column: zero, minus one; two, zero. That single matrix sends the grid exactly where the two moves did.

Swap the order, rotate first and then stretch, and you get a different matrix: zero, minus two; one, zero. The grids do not match. Matrix multiplication does not commute, because doing things in a different order is a different thing. That is why a network's layers cannot be reordered.

Section 4 ▶ watch 2:17

Determinant and inverse

How much does the machine stretch area? Follow the unit square. Under our matrix it becomes a parallelogram of area two. That number has a formula, a d minus b c, and a name: the determinant. Here, two times one, minus one times zero. Two.

If the determinant is zero, the square is squashed flat onto a line. Area gone. And once it is gone, nothing can bring it back: a matrix with determinant zero has no inverse. For any other, the inverse is the machine run backwards, and it returns every point home.

Section 5 ▶ watch 2:51

Eigenvectors: the directions that only stretch

Most arrows get turned by a matrix. A few do not. They stay on their own line and only get longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. A times v equals lambda times v.

Take the symmetric matrix two, one, one, two. To find lambda, ask when A minus lambda I squashes space: its determinant must be zero. Two minus lambda, squared, minus one, equals zero. So lambda is three, or one. For three, the direction one, one: the matrix sends it to three, three. For one, the direction one, minus one, which it leaves alone.

Here is why this matters for training. The bowl from lesson one, x squared plus three y squared, has a matrix of second derivatives: two, zero, zero, six. Its eigenvalues are the curvatures along the eigenvector directions: two along x, six along y. And the steepest curvature sets the learning-rate limit we found then: two divided by six, one third. The exact same number, from the eigenvalue.

Section 6 ▶ watch 3:57

Inside a neural network and a Transformer

Every layer of a neural network is a matrix. The input vector goes in, W times x plus b comes out, and training is gradient descent on the entries of W. In GPT-two, each of the 12 layers holds a matrix of 768 by 3072: 2,359,296 learned numbers in one matrix, and 124 million parameters in all.

Attention is a matrix product too. Stack the query vectors as rows of Q, the key vectors as rows of K, and Q times K transpose computes every query-key dot product at once: a thousand tokens give a thousand by thousand grid of scores in one operation. That is why it runs so fast on a graphics processor, and why the 12 heads of each layer can run side by side.

And the data itself is a matrix. A chest C.T. slice is 512 by 512 numbers, 262,144 pixel intensities. A convolution slides a small matrix across it, and the library rewrites that as one big matrix product, so the same hardware that trains GPT-two reads the scan.

Industry: search, recommendation and cheap fine-tuning

Google ranks web pages with an eigenvector. Build a matrix where each page passes its importance to the pages it links to, add a little damping, and the ranking is the vector that the matrix sends to itself. Apply the matrix again and again, and any starting guess settles onto it. Here, six pages: page three is linked by everyone, and the eigenvector says so.

Recommendation is a matrix problem. Netflix's prize dataset had 100.48 million ratings from 480,189 users of 17,770 films: a table of 8.5 billion cells with only 1.2 percent filled. The winning idea was to write the giant table as a product of two thin ones, users times hidden tastes, and tastes times films. Any matrix splits this way, into a sum of simple layers, each a column times a row, weighted by a singular value.

Keep the biggest few layers and you keep most of the structure. Here is a forty-eight by forty-eight image: 2,304 numbers. Six layers rebuild it with 582. The same trick, called LoRA, is how large language models are fine-tuned today: instead of changing a 768 by 768 weight matrix, 589,824 numbers, you learn two thin matrices of rank 8, 12,288 numbers, 48 times fewer.

Section 8 ▶ watch 6:26

Recap: what, where in AI, and how to use it

So. A matrix moves space; its columns say where the basis lands. Multiplying matrices is composing moves, and order matters. The determinant is the area factor. Eigenvectors are the directions that only stretch, and their eigenvalues are the curvatures that limit how fast you can learn.

Where in A.I.? Every layer is a matrix, attention is a matrix product, every image is a matrix, and fine-tuning is a low-rank matrix. How to use it: read a matrix by its columns, check the determinant before you invert, and let the largest eigenvalue set your learning rate. Assessment

Answer each question in your own words, then reveal the model answer. Every answer is computed from the lesson's verified mathematics.

How are matrices used in neural networks?

Show answer
Each layer computes W times x plus b: a weight matrix times the input vector. GPT-2's feed-forward layers are 768-by-3072 matrices, about 2.4 million learned numbers each, and the model's 124 million parameters are a few hundred such matrices.

How does attention use matrices?

Show answer
Stack the queries as rows of Q and the keys as rows of K; Q times K transpose gives every query-key dot product in one product, a token-by-token grid of scores. Each of the 12 heads in a GPT-2 layer does this in parallel on the GPU.

What does a matrix do to a vector?

Show answer
It mixes the vector's coordinates with the matrix's columns: the result is the first coordinate times the first column plus the second coordinate times the second column. Geometrically, the whole space moves and the vector moves with it.

Why is matrix multiplication done the way it is?

Show answer
Because multiplying two matrices is the same as applying one transformation and then the other. The row-times-column rule is exactly what composition requires, and that is also why the order matters.

What are eigenvectors and why do they matter for training?

Show answer
Directions the matrix does not turn, only stretches; the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. For a loss surface they are the principal curvatures, and the largest eigenvalue sets the maximum safe learning rate.

What is LoRA?

Show answer
Low-rank adaptation: instead of updating a full 768-by-768 weight matrix (589,824 numbers), learn two thin matrices of rank 8 (12,288 numbers), 48 times fewer. Any matrix can be approximated by a sum of a few rank-one layers, which is the same idea behind image compression and recommender systems.

Where else are matrices used in industry?

Show answer
Google's PageRank is the principal eigenvector of the web's link matrix; Netflix-style recommenders factorise the user-by-film ratings matrix into two thin ones; every CT slice is a 512-by-512 matrix that a convolutional network reads as matrix products.
